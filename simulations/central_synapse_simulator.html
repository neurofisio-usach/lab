Central Synapse — Voltage-Clamp EPSC Simulator

A small population of release sites (2–4), AMPA and NMDA receptor currents, and Mg2+ block that lifts as the cell is depolarized. Recorded as a whole-cell voltage-clamp current, the way it would look on a real amplifier — contrast with the many-release-site, high-Pr regime at the neuromuscular junction.

How this simulation works

Each stimulus is delivered to 3 independent release sites, each releasing one vesicle with probability Pr after a ~1 ms synaptic delay (presynaptic Ca2+ channel opening, vesicle fusion, diffusion across the cleft, and receptor binding — Sabatini & Regehr, 1996). Released glutamate activates both AMPA and NMDA receptors on the same postsynaptic density: the AMPA current is fast (rise ~0.3 ms, decay ~3 ms) and the NMDA current is slow (rise ~5 ms, decay ~80 ms) and voltage-dependent, since extracellular Mg2+ blocks the NMDA channel pore at hyperpolarized potentials and unblocks as the membrane depolarizes (Jahr & Stevens relation). The recorded current is I(t) = g(t) · (Vhold − 0 mV), so at very negative holding potentials the trace deflects sharply downward (inward current), and near 0 mV the driving force — and the current — shrinks toward zero even though channels are open.

Unlike the neuromuscular junction's single terminal with hundreds of release sites, a central synaptic connection typically involves only a handful of contacts, so failures (0 quanta released) are common even without lowering calcium — this is the everyday operating regime of a cortical or hippocampal synapse, not a special experimental condition.

In the Paired-Pulse / Train tab, each release site depletes after releasing and recovers over hundreds of milliseconds, while each release event also transiently raises release probability at the remaining sites (facilitation). At low Pr, facilitation dominates the paired-pulse ratio; at higher Pr, depletion-driven depression takes over — the same rule of thumb described by Dobrunz & Stevens (1997).

Because a connection only has 2–4 release sites, the paired-pulse ratio itself is a noisy statistic: with so few sites, whether a given site happens to still be available for pulse 2 is close to a coin flip, and averaging a modest number of trials doesn't fully wash that out — a fresh "average" run can genuinely land on facilitation one time and depression the next, purely from sampling variability, even with every slider held fixed. This is a real feature of small-N synapses (and part of why single-connection experiments need many repeats), not a simulator error. Averaging more trials narrows the spread but only slowly, since it shrinks with the square root of trial count.

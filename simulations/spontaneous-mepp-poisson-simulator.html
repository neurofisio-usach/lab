Spontaneous Miniature End-Plate Potentials

A resting frog end-plate, recorded with no nerve stimulation, after Fatt & Katz (1952). Small potentials of roughly half a millivolt appear at irregular moments: single quanta released at random. Are they really random? Record from four junctions and test whether the intervals between events follow an exponential distribution and the number of events per time window follows a Poisson distribution. One junction behaves like a Poisson process; the other mystery junctions may not.

How this simulation works

Fatt & Katz (1952) recorded from frog muscle fibres at the end-plate with no nerve stimulation and saw small spontaneous potentials, now called miniature end-plate potentials (mEPPs). They showed that the intervals between them were distributed exponentially, as expected if each quantum is released independently of the others at a constant average rate. That is the definition of a Poisson process.

A Poisson process with rate λ has two equivalent signatures. The waiting time between events is exponential, p(t) = λ·e^(−λt), with mean 1/λ and SD also 1/λ, so the coefficient of variation is CV = 1. The number of events in a window of length Δt follows a Poisson distribution, P(k) = e^(−μ)·μ^k / k! with μ = λ·Δt, whose variance equals its mean, so the Fano factor is 1. On a logarithmic y-axis the exponential interval histogram becomes a straight line with slope −λ.

In Explore you set λ yourself and the process is always Poisson. Junctions A, B and C have hidden rates of about one event per second. Exactly one of them is a Poisson process. Another fires too regularly: its intervals are gamma-distributed, like a noisy clock. The third releases in clusters: bursts of several quanta at short intervals, separated by long silences. All three have similar mean rates, so the rate alone cannot tell them apart; only the shape of the distributions can. The assignment and the rates are randomized on page load. Typing the same group code always reproduces the same junctions and the same data.

Recording noise is Gaussian (SD ≈ 0.025 mV) and mEPP amplitudes vary around a quantal size near 0.5 mV. Every event is detected, so the analysis uses the true event times. The χ² test groups count classes so that each has at least 5 expected windows; the degrees of freedom are the number of classes minus 2, because μ is estimated from the same data. The CSV export contains event times and amplitudes, for analysis in a spreadsheet or Python.

Things to try:

  1. In Explore, record a few hundred events. Which interval is most common, the shortest or the mean? Does a random train look evenly spaced in the raster?
  2. Turn on the log y-axis. Read the slope of the line and compare it with 1/mean interval.
  3. Change the bin width and the window Δt. Which conclusions change, and which do not?
  4. Record about 1000 s from each junction. Use CV, Fano factor, the shape of both histograms and the χ² test to decide which junction is Poisson, which is regular and which is clustered. Then reveal the answer.
  5. With only 30 s of data, can you still tell them apart? How much recording do you need?

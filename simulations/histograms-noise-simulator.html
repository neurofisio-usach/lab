Histograms and Noise: Seeing Current Levels

An amplitude histogram is our estimate of a probability distribution, and how we build it changes what we see. The first exercise is about the histogram itself: bin width, bin position and normalization. The second generates a patch with one or more channels in Gaussian noise and asks how well the current levels can be resolved, how to measure the unitary current and the open probability by fitting Gaussians, and whether the channels open independently of each other.

Group code

Patches A, B and C contain current amplitudes with one, two or three levels, in an order you don't know. For each patch, decide how many levels there are. Change the bin width and the bin position and watch the shape change. Which conclusions survive every reasonable binning?

A peak counts as "apparent" if it holds at least 10% of the tallest bin and rises above the dip separating it from any taller peak by more than √count. The count in each bin is itself a random number with SD ≈ √count, so narrow bins with few events produce peaks that are only noise.

How this simulation works

Bin width. Patches A, B and C are drawn from a single Gaussian, from two Gaussians whose means are about 2.5 SD apart, and from three levels of unequal weight; the assignment changes with the group code. "Open times" are exponential. The rules of thumb balance two errors: wide bins blur real structure, narrow bins are dominated by counting noise. Sturges' rule assumes Gaussian data and gives too few bins for large n; Scott's rule uses the SD; Freedman–Diaconis uses the interquartile range and is more robust to tails and multiple peaks. With counts the height depends on n and on bin width; with probability density (count / (n · bin width)) the area is 1 and histograms with different n or bins can be compared.

Resolving levels. Each channel switches between closed and open with rates chosen so that its open probability is Po and its mean open time is the selected value, simulated every 0.05 ms (20 kHz) for 2 s. White Gaussian noise of the chosen SD is added and the record is filtered with a Gaussian digital filter (σ of the impulse response = 0.1325 / fc). Filtering reduces the noise but also rounds the transitions and shrinks openings shorter than about 1/(2fc), which then fill the space between the peaks. With "gate independently" off, a channel opens four times faster for each other channel already open (positive cooperativity), and the level occupancies no longer follow the binomial.

Fitting. The fit is a mixture of Gaussians estimated by maximum likelihood (expectation–maximization) on all 40 000 samples. The unitary current is the slope of a straight line through the fitted means against level number; Po is the mean current divided by N·i, where N is the number of Gaussians minus one. The misclassification rate counts samples assigned to the wrong number of open channels when thresholds are placed halfway between levels, using the true i for your patch and the fitted i for the mystery patch. Mystery patches have 1–3 channels with i between 1.2 and 2.5 pA, set by the group code.

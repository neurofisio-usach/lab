From One Recording to Many: Sampling Statistics

Every number we measure in the lab, a unitary current, an open time, an EPSC amplitude, is one draw from a population we never see in full. These six exercises show what a sample can and cannot tell you about that population: how means and variances are estimated, what an error bar means, why averages become Gaussian, why a bar graph can hide the data, and how many measurements an experiment needs.

How this simulation works

1 · Estimate μ and σ². Amplitudes are drawn from a Gaussian with a hidden mean (1.8–2.4 pA) and SD (0.25–0.40 pA), randomized per group code. The sum of squared deviations from the sample mean is always a little smaller than from the true mean, so dividing it by n underestimates σ² by the factor (n − 1)/n. Dividing by n − 1 removes that bias. The difference matters for n = 3 and is negligible for n = 500.

2 · SD vs SEM. The SD describes how much single measurements vary and does not shrink with more data. The SEM, SD/√n, estimates how much the mean would vary between repeated experiments. About 68% of ±SEM bars and 95% of 95% confidence intervals contain μ. The CI uses the t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom, which matters for small n. On the log–log plot the SEM line has slope −½.

3 · Central limit theorem. Open times are exponential (τ = 2 ms), counts are Poisson (μ = 1.5), two current levels are a mixture of closed (60%, 0 pA) and open (40%, 2 pA) with Gaussian noise. Whatever the population, the mean of n measurements has SD σ/√n, and its distribution approaches a Gaussian as n grows; its skewness falls as 1/√n. This is why tests on means work even when single measurements are far from Gaussian.

4 · Show the data. The four groups are generated from a Gaussian, a skewed (log-normal), a bimodal (two subpopulations of cells) and a Gaussian-plus-outlier distribution, then rescaled so that every group has mean 40 pA and SD 18 pA exactly. The bar graph is therefore identical for all four. Medians, ranges and the plots themselves are not.

5 · Bootstrap. Open times are exponential with a hidden τ (1.5–3 ms). Each bootstrap resample draws n values from your own sample with replacement and recomputes the mean. The spread of those 2000 means estimates the standard error, and their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a confidence interval that follows the skewed shape of the true sampling distribution. "Check 200 experiments" measures how often each kind of interval actually contains the true τ.

6 · How many cells? Control cells have mean 20 pA; drug cells have mean 20 + Δ pA; both have the chosen SD. Each experiment is analysed with Welch's two-sample t-test. Power depends only on the effect size d = Δ/SD and on n. With Δ = 0 every "significant" result is a false positive, and p-values are uniformly distributed, so about 5% fall below 0.05.

Dwell Times: Exponential Waiting and Log-Binned Histograms

How long a channel stays open is a random waiting time. If leaving the open state has a constant probability per unit time, open times follow an exponential distribution with time constant τ. These two exercises show how to read such distributions: the linear histogram, the survivor plot, the memoryless property, the bias from brief events the recording misses, and the log-binned histogram of Sigworth & Sine (1987) that separates kinetic components differing by orders of magnitude.

Given that a channel has already stayed open for s, how much longer will it stay open? For an exponential the answer does not depend on s: the remaining time has the same distribution and the same mean τ as a brand-new opening.

Many channels have several open or closed states, so dwell times are a sum of exponentials whose time constants can differ by 100-fold. On a linear axis you can see either the fast or the slow component, never both. With logarithmic bins and a square-root ordinate each component becomes a peak located at its own τ. Design a channel, or record from a mystery channel and find out how many components it has.

Adding a component always increases the log-likelihood. Keep it only if the gain is large: under the simpler model, 2·ΔlogL is approximately χ² with 2 degrees of freedom (one extra τ and one extra weight), so 2·ΔlogL > 6 corresponds to p < 0.05.

How this simulation works

Exponential waiting times. Each opening lasts −τ·ln(U), with U uniform between 0 and 1, which gives the density f(t) = (1/τ)·e^(−t/τ). Its mean and SD both equal τ (CV = 1) and its most frequent value is the shortest one. The survivor function, the fraction of openings longer than t, is S(t) = e^(−t/τ), a straight line of slope −1/τ on a log y-axis. The record at the top shows the first openings of the dataset separated by closed times drawn from a second exponential, with a little current noise; it is a picture of the data, not a filtered recording.

Dead time. Openings shorter than the dead time are dropped. Because the exponential is memoryless, the detected openings minus the dead time are again exponential with the same τ, so the maximum-likelihood estimate is simply mean − dead time. The plain mean is biased upwards. The survivor slope and the median are not biased by missed events if the dead time is subtracted, but they are noisier than the maximum-likelihood estimate.

Log-binning. With bins of equal width in log t, the expected count in a bin is proportional to t·f(t), which for each exponential component peaks at t = τ. Plotting √count makes the random scatter roughly the same in every bin (Poisson counts have variance equal to their mean, and the square root stabilizes that variance), so small and large peaks can be judged by eye. Sigworth & Sine (1987, Biophys J 52:1047) recommend about 10 bins per decade; much coarser binning distorts the shape.

Fitting. The fit maximizes the likelihood of the individual dwell times (no binning) for a mixture of 1, 2 or 3 exponentials, using the expectation–maximization algorithm. Mystery channels have 2 or 3 components with time constants near 0.2–0.6 ms, 2–6 ms and 20–60 ms; these, and the τ of the mystery channel in tab 1, are set by the group code.
